# Reader brief: one radiograph, is a fracture visible?

You are shown one X-ray image and nothing else: no history, no age, no report, no earlier images.
Read the image only, and answer one question about it. Most of these images are of the wrist,
many of them of children; some are of the hand, leg, hip or shoulder of adults.

rAidiology is not a medical device. This answer is a measurement of how well a careful general
reader does on one image; it is never advice to anybody about their own scan.

## How to look

Work through the image in the same order every time, so nothing is skipped because something else
caught the eye first.

1. **Every cortical margin.** The cortex is the bright outer shell of each bone. Follow it all the
   way round every bone in the picture, both sides, end to end, as if tracing it with a pencil.
   A fracture shows as a **step** (the line jumps sideways), a **buckle** (a small bulge or kink
   where the bone has been squashed, common in children and easy to miss because nothing is broken
   right through), or a **break** (a gap in the line). Look at the bone next to the obvious one too:
   one fracture often has a second.
2. **A line crossing bone.** A dark (lucent) line running across or into the bone where none
   should be is a fracture line. A bright (dense) band can be one as well, where two pieces have
   been pushed into each other. Normal lines are smooth, expected and usually paired on both sides
   (growth plates, nutrient canals, overlapping edges of neighbouring bones); a fracture line is
   where it should not be and often sharp or jagged.
3. **The distal radius and ulna.** Since most images are of the wrist, look hardest here: the ends
   of the two forearm bones just above the wrist. Check the cortex on the thumb side, the little
   finger side, the front and the back; check the tip of the ulna (the ulnar styloid), which breaks
   off often; check the angle of the radius's joint surface on a side view, since a tilt backwards
   or forwards means it has broken and moved.
4. **The growth plates in a child.** In a child the ends of long bones are joined to the shaft by
   a dark band of cartilage, the growth plate (physis). That band is normal and is not a fracture.
   What matters is its shape: a plate that is **wider than its partner or uneven along its length**,
   or a bone end that has **slipped** off the shaft, means the injury went through the plate, and
   that is a fracture. A small chip of bone at the corner of the plate counts too.
5. **Joint alignment.** Bones that meet at a joint should line up and keep an even gap. A joint
   that is out of line, or a bone that sits off its partner, often goes with a fracture nearby.
6. **Soft tissue.** Swelling of the soft tissue over a bone, or a fat line pushed out of place
   next to a joint (a sign of fluid in the joint, an effusion), points to an injury underneath.
   On its own it is a reason to look again, very closely, at the bone beside it.

## Casts and metal

A plaster cast, a splint, screws, plates or wires mean somebody already found something and
treated it. That is a clue about the history, not itself a fracture. Look through the cast or
around the metal at the bone and decide whether a fracture is **visible on this image**. A healing
fracture still visible as a line, a step or a lump of new bone (callus) is a yes.

## The answer

The answer is a yes or a no about **this image**: is a fracture visible on it? Not whether the
person might have one somewhere else, and not whether it is old or new.

An uncertain yes is still a yes. If you see something you think is probably a fracture, say yes
and give a low confidence; do not say no because you are unsure. Say no only when you have looked
at every margin and found nothing.
